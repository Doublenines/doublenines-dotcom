99 Sprints: What We Learned Building Coop's Truck Sharing Platform

December 1, 2024

Coop is the trucking industry's first and only asset sharing platform — and it was built by a senior, AI-fluent team we placed and embedded with Coop for nearly two years. Ninety-nine bi-weekly sprints, building something that had never been built before. Here's what the team learned along the way.

Building for an Industry That Doesn't Trust Software

The trucking industry is not Silicon Valley. Fleet operators have been burned by overpromising tech vendors before. They run on relationships, phone calls, and handshake deals. The biggest challenge for the team we placed wasn't technical — it was earning trust from an industry deeply skeptical of yet another platform claiming to solve their problems.

The team learned early that the only way to earn that trust was to build something that worked exactly the way fleet operators already think — not a Silicon Valley abstraction of trucking, but the actual, messy, nuanced way that truck sharing happens in the real world. That kind of judgment is why Coop brought on senior people from day one instead of a junior build team.

The Power of a Senior Team That Stays

99 sprints is a lot of sprints. A typical staffing arrangement churns people in and out long before that. But building a marketplace from scratch — especially in an industry that's never had one — requires sustained commitment from people who carry the context forward. The product shipped in sprint 99 looks nothing like sprint 1, and that continuity is exactly the point.

Some key milestones along the way:

  • Sprints 1-15: Core marketplace — listing assets, matching supply with demand, basic booking flow
  • Sprints 16-35: Trust and verification — fleet verification, insurance integration, dispute resolution
  • Sprints 36-60: Operational tooling — trip management, maintenance tracking, real-time GPS integration
  • Sprints 61-80: Financial infrastructure — automated billing, payment processing, revenue analytics
  • Sprints 81-99: Scale and optimization — performance tuning, advanced matching algorithms, enterprise API integrations

Pivots Are Not Failures

The team pivoted the core product model three times during those 99 sprints. Each pivot felt uncomfortable in the moment, but in retrospect each one was essential. The market taught the team things no one could have known at the whiteboard.

The biggest pivot came around sprint 40, when it became clear the marketplace needed to shift from pure peer-to-peer to a managed marketplace with Coop playing an active operational role. Fleet operators didn't just want a platform to find trucks — they wanted someone to handle the logistics of sharing them. That insight reshaped the entire product. Senior people spotted it because they'd seen similar patterns before.

What 99 Sprints Teaches You

Embedding a senior team on a single product for this long changes how you think about software development:

  1. Velocity compounds. Sprint 80 was dramatically more productive than sprint 8 — not because the team worked harder, but because they'd built the right foundations, understood the domain deeply, and earned everyone's trust.
  2. The best features come from observation. The most impactful features weren't on any roadmap. They came from watching fleet operators use the platform and noticing where they struggled.
  3. Technical debt is real, but manageable. Across 99 sprints the team refactored core systems multiple times. The key is doing it proactively, before debt becomes a crisis.
  4. Relationships matter more than process. Process evolved constantly, but the trust between the placed team and Coop's team stayed consistent. That's what made the hard decisions possible.

The People Who Made It Happen

99 sprints doesn't happen without exceptional leadership on both sides. On the Coop side, Karen Jones (CMO) brought relentless clarity to the product vision and kept the team focused on what fleet operators actually needed. John Diez (CFO) ensured every feature had a clear path to revenue impact — the kind of discipline that separates products that ship from products that stall. And Romain Rousseau was instrumental in bridging business strategy and technical execution, keeping both teams aligned through every pivot and course correction.

The best engagements aren't transactional — they're partnerships. Placing senior people who stay long enough to own the outcome is how you get there.
