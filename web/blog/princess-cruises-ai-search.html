How We Built AI-Powered Search for Princess Cruises

October 27, 2025

When Princess Cruises approached us, they had a classic search problem: thousands of itineraries across dozens of destinations, and customers who didn't know exactly what they were looking for. Traditional keyword search wasn't cutting it — travelers don't search for "7-night Western Caribbean sailing departing Port Canaveral." They search for "warm beach trip in January that's good for families."

The Challenge

Princess Cruises' catalog includes thousands of sailings with complex attributes — itinerary, ship class, ports of call, onboard amenities, pricing tiers, seasonal availability, and more. The existing search experience relied on rigid filters that forced users to already know what they wanted before they could find it.

Our goal: build a natural language search experience that understands intent, not just keywords.

Our Approach

We started by mapping the most common ways travelers describe their ideal cruise — not in database terms, but in human terms. Things like:

  • "Something romantic for our anniversary in the Mediterranean"
  • "A family cruise with lots of kids activities under $3,000"
  • "Alaska cruise with glacier viewing, departing from Seattle"
  • "Short weekend getaway from Miami"

From there, we built a pipeline that combines semantic search with structured filtering. The natural language query gets processed by an LLM that extracts intent, preferences, and constraints. Those get mapped to structured attributes in the catalog, and results are ranked by semantic relevance — not just keyword match.

Technical Architecture: Next.js and the Latest AI Tools

We built the search experience on Next.js, leveraging its server-side rendering capabilities to ensure fast initial page loads and SEO-friendly results pages. The architecture takes full advantage of Next.js API routes for the AI pipeline, server components for streaming search results, and edge functions for low-latency query processing.

The system integrates the latest AI tools across the stack:

  • Query understanding layer — GPT-4 and Claude parse natural language into structured intent (destination preferences, travel dates, budget range, party composition, activity interests), with model selection based on query complexity
  • Hybrid retrieval engine — Combines vector similarity search (using OpenAI embeddings) on cruise descriptions with traditional database filtering on hard constraints (dates, pricing, availability)
  • Ranking and personalization — Results are re-ranked based on relevance confidence, with AI-generated explanations for why each result matches the query

Interface Design: Making AI Invisible

The biggest interface design challenge was making AI-powered search feel natural, not technical. Travelers shouldn't need to know they're using AI — they should just feel like the search finally understands them.

We designed the search interface around progressive disclosure: a clean, prominent search bar that accepts plain English, with results that surface relevant itinerary details dynamically based on what the user asked about. If someone searches for family-friendly options, the results cards emphasize kids' programs and family staterooms. If they search by price, the pricing information gets top billing.

This required close collaboration between our design team and the AI engineering team — every AI capability needed a corresponding UI pattern that communicated results clearly without overwhelming users.

Staffing with Senior Talent

A project of this complexity demanded senior talent across every discipline. We staffed the engagement with experienced Next.js engineers who'd built production AI interfaces before, senior designers with deep expertise in search UX and information architecture, and AI engineers who understood both the capabilities and limitations of the latest models.

The combination of senior-level interface design thinking and hands-on AI engineering experience is what made it possible to ship a production system — not just a demo — in a compressed timeline. This is the kind of talent profile we've since formalized through Fuerza Talent.

Performance Management at Scale

AI-powered search introduces performance challenges that traditional search doesn't have. LLM inference adds latency. Embedding lookups require vector database optimization. And streaming results to the frontend needs careful orchestration to feel responsive.

We implemented several performance management strategies:

  • Query caching — Common and similar queries share cached results, reducing redundant LLM calls by over 60%
  • Streaming responses — Search results appear progressively as the AI pipeline processes them, so users see initial results within milliseconds
  • Performance monitoring — Real-time dashboards track p50/p95 latencies, cache hit rates, and AI model response times, with automated alerts for degradation
  • Graceful degradation — If AI services are slow or unavailable, the system falls back to enhanced keyword search so users are never blocked

Results

The AI-powered search experience launched to positive reception from both Princess Cruises' internal teams and their customers. Early metrics showed meaningful improvements in search-to-booking conversion rates and a significant reduction in customer service inquiries about finding specific itineraries.

More importantly, the system handles the ambiguous, conversational queries that traditional search simply can't — which is exactly how most travelers actually think about planning a cruise.

Lessons Learned

Building AI search for a real enterprise product taught us several things we now apply across our engagements:

  1. Start with user language, not data structure. The most important step was studying how real travelers describe their ideal trip — then working backward to the data model.
  2. Hybrid beats pure AI. Pure semantic search produces impressive demos but unreliable production results. Combining AI understanding with structured filtering gives you the best of both worlds.
  3. Explainability matters. Users trust search results more when they can see why each result was recommended. "This matches because you mentioned family activities and Caribbean" builds confidence.
  4. Invest in performance from day one. AI latency is real, and users have zero tolerance for slow search. Performance management needs to be a first-class concern, not an afterthought.
  5. Staff with senior talent. AI projects that ship to production need engineers and designers who've done it before. Junior teams produce impressive prototypes but struggle with the edge cases, performance tuning, and interface nuance that production demands.
